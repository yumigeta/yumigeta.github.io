Graphene Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectroscopy is the go-to tool for characterizing graphene: it identifies the number of layers, quantifies defects, and measures strain and doping — all from a quick, non-destructive optical measurement.

00 — Phonon dispersion

Six phonon branches from two atoms

Graphene's unit cell has two carbon atoms, giving rise to six phonon branches: three acoustic (A) and three optical (O). Each is further classified as in-plane longitudinal (L), in-plane transverse (T), or out-of-plane (Z). The Raman-active modes live near the $\Gamma$ point (first-order) and the K point (double resonance).

Phonon dispersion of graphene along $\Gamma \to M \to K \to \Gamma$. The iTO branch near K (~1350 cm−1) gives rise to the D and 2D bands. The iLO and iTO branches at Γ (~1580 cm−1) produce the G band. Hover over the branches to identify them.

01 — The G band

First-order Raman scattering at ~1580 cm−1

The G band is the only first-order Raman peak in graphene. It originates from the doubly degenerate $E_{2g}$ phonon mode at the Brillouin zone center ($\Gamma$ point), where adjacent carbon atoms vibrate against each other in-plane.

$E_{2g}$ vibration at $\Gamma$

Scattering process

First-order process: an incident photon creates one phonon at $\Gamma$ ($\mathbf{q} \approx 0$) and is scattered. Because this is a zone-center mode, the G band frequency (~1580 cm−1) is independent of laser wavelength. The G band is present in all sp2 carbon materials — graphene, graphite, carbon nanotubes, and amorphous carbon.
Why "G"? The G stands for "graphite." This was the first Raman peak identified in graphitic materials, and the name has persisted even as the field shifted to graphene.
02 — The 2D band

Double resonance at ~2700 cm−1

The 2D band (also called G′) is the most prominent second-order peak. It involves two iTO phonons near K with opposite momenta ($\mathbf{q}$ and $-\mathbf{q}$), produced through a double resonance process — making it strongly dependent on laser energy and uniquely sensitive to the number of graphene layers.

The double resonance process for the 2D band. An electron–hole pair is created near K, the electron is scattered by an iTO phonon to K′, then scattered back by a second iTO phonon, and recombines emitting a photon. Both phonon scattering events are inter-valley (K → K′ → K).

Why is the 2D band so useful?
  • Layer counting: In monolayer graphene, the 2D band is a single sharp Lorentzian (~24 cm−1 FWHM) with intensity much larger than G. In bilayer, it splits into four components due to the splitting of electronic bands. In few-layer graphene, the shape evolves further — making the 2D lineshape a reliable fingerprint for layer number.
  • Dispersive: The 2D frequency shifts ~100 cm$^{-1}$/eV with laser energy because the resonant phonon wavevector $\mathbf{q}$ depends on the electronic band structure.
  • No defects needed: Unlike the D band, momentum is conserved by the two phonons ($\mathbf{q} + (-\mathbf{q}) = 0$), so the 2D band appears in pristine graphene.
Why "2D" and not "G′"? The 2D band involves two phonons of the same type as the D band (iTO near K), hence its frequency is roughly twice the D band frequency. Both names are used in the literature — "2D" emphasizes the phonon origin, while "G′" avoids confusion with implying defect involvement.
03 — The D band

Defect-activated double resonance at ~1350 cm−1

The D band involves the same iTO phonon near K as the 2D band, but only one phonon participates — momentum conservation is completed by elastic scattering from a defect. This is why the D band is absent in pristine graphene and serves as a direct measure of disorder.

The double resonance process for the D band. An electron is excited near K, scattered to K′ by an iTO phonon, then elastically scattered back by a defect, and recombines. The defect provides the missing momentum — no defect, no D band.

Quantifying defects: The intensity ratio I(D)/I(G) is widely used to quantify defect density. For low defect concentrations, I(D)/I(G) increases with defect density. At very high defect concentrations, both peaks broaden and the ratio decreases — the sp2 structure itself is breaking down. The relationship follows the Tuinstra–Koenig relation: $I(D)/I(G) \propto 1/L_a$, where $L_a$ is the average distance between defects (crystallite size).
D′ and D+D′ bands. The D′ band (~1620 cm−1) is another defect-activated peak, but involves intra-valley scattering (within the same K point). The D+D′ combination band (~2940 cm−1) involves one iTO phonon (inter-valley) and one iLO phonon (intra-valley), also requiring defects.
04 — Reading a graphene Raman spectrum

What each peak tells you

A single Raman measurement encodes layer number, crystal quality, strain state, and charge doping. Here is how to extract each.

Layer number

  • Monolayer: single symmetric 2D peak, $I(2D)/I(G) > 1$
  • Bilayer: 2D splits into 4 Lorentzians, $I(2D)/I(G) \sim 1$
  • Few-layer: 2D broadens asymmetrically, $I(2D)/I(G) < 1$
  • Graphite: 2D has two components ($2D_1$, $2D_2$)

Defects & disorder

  • $I(D)/I(G) \approx 0$: pristine graphene
  • $I(D)/I(G)$ increases: more point defects or edges
  • D′ appears: confirms defect activation
  • $I(D)/I(D')$: distinguishes vacancy (~7), boundary (~3.5), sp$^3$ (~13) defect types

Strain

  • Both G and 2D peaks shift under strain
  • Tensile: red-shift (lower frequency)
  • Compressive: blue-shift (higher frequency)
  • G band splits under uniaxial strain ($G^+$ and $G^-$)
  • Slope in $\Delta\omega_G$ vs $\Delta\omega_{2D}$ plot: ~2.2 for strain

Doping

  • G band stiffens (blue-shifts) with both n- and p-doping
  • G band narrows due to Pauli blocking of phonon decay
  • 2D band shifts down for n-type, up for p-type
  • Slope in $\Delta\omega_G$ vs $\Delta\omega_{2D}$ plot: ~0.7 for doping
  • Distinguishes strain from doping via correlation plot
The strain–doping correlation plot. Plotting $\Delta\omega_G$ vs $\Delta\omega_{2D}$ separates strain and doping effects: pure strain follows a slope of ~2.2, while pure doping gives ~0.7. Real samples often show a combination — the data point's position relative to these two axes reveals both contributions simultaneously. This technique, introduced by Lee et al. (2012), is now a standard tool in graphene characterization.
05 — From 2D graphene to nanoribbons

How quantum confinement reshapes the Raman spectrum

Cut graphene into a narrow strip — a graphene nanoribbon (GNR) — and the Raman spectrum changes dramatically. The finite width quantizes transverse momenta, creates new low-frequency modes, and activates edge-related scattering. Raman spectroscopy can identify the ribbon width, edge geometry, and electronic character.

Why the spectrum changes

In 2D graphene, phonon wavevectors span a continuous Brillouin zone. In a nanoribbon of width $W$, the transverse component is quantized: only momenta $q_\perp = n\pi/W$ survive ($n = 1, 2, \ldots$). This is the same zone-folding picture as for electronic subbands — but applied to phonons. Each allowed $q_\perp$ "slices" the 2D phonon dispersion, producing a family of discrete 1D phonon subbands.

Radial breathing-like mode (RBLM)

The most distinctive new feature is the RBLM — a low-frequency mode (~100–600 cm−1) where the ribbon width oscillates in-phase, analogous to the radial breathing mode in carbon nanotubes. Its frequency scales inversely with width:

$$\omega_\text{RBLM} \approx \frac{3222}{W} \;\text{(cm}^{-1}\!\cdot\!\text{Å)}$$

This provides a direct, non-destructive width measurement — the narrower the ribbon, the higher the RBLM frequency. For a 1 nm ribbon, $\omega_\text{RBLM} \approx 322$ cm$^{-1}$; for a 5 nm ribbon, it drops to ~64 cm$^{-1}$.

G band in nanoribbons

The G band persists but is modified by confinement:

  • The $E_{2g}$ degeneracy is lifted — the G band can split into $G^+$ (along the ribbon axis) and $G^-$ (transverse), separated by a few cm$^{-1}$
  • For very narrow ribbons (< 5 nm), both components shift and the splitting becomes resolvable
  • The splitting and shift depend on edge geometry (armchair vs. zigzag) and width

D band and edge activation

Edges act as line defects that break translational symmetry — exactly the kind of elastic scattering needed for the D band. In nanoribbons:

  • The D band is always present, even in defect-free ribbons, because every edge provides the momentum $q_\text{defect}$ for double resonance
  • $I(D)/I(G)$ scales as $\sim 1/W$ — narrower ribbons have a larger edge-to-area ratio, so the D band dominates
  • Edge type matters: armchair edges efficiently activate the D band (inter-valley scattering), while perfect zigzag edges do not (they lack the K→K′ momentum transfer). The D band intensity thus distinguishes edge chirality
  • The D′ band (~1620 cm−1), requiring only intra-valley scattering, is activated by both edge types

2D band: still present, but changed

The 2D band does not require defects (two phonons conserve momentum), so it persists in nanoribbons. However, quantum confinement modifies which phonon wavevectors contribute to the double resonance, and the electronic density of states develops van Hove singularities that enhance specific resonance channels. The result: the 2D band shape and intensity change with width, providing additional confirmation of confinement effects.

Summary: graphene vs. nanoribbon Raman

Feature 2D Graphene Nanoribbon (GNR)
RBLM absent present — $\omega \propto 1/W$
G band single peak ~1580 cm−1 split into $G^+$/$G^-$
D band defect-activated only always present (edges)
I(D)/I(G) $\approx 0$ (pristine) $\propto 1/W$
2D band single Lorentzian (1L) modified by subband structure
Low-freq. modes none (Raman-active) quantized phonon subbands
Connection to carbon nanotubes. Rolling a nanoribbon into a tube adds periodic boundary conditions instead of hard-wall ones, giving the same zone-folding physics. The CNT radial breathing mode (RBM, $\omega \approx 248/d$ cm$^{-1}$) is the rolled-up counterpart of the RBLM. G band splitting into $G^+$/$G^-$ occurs in both systems, and the D band serves as a defect/edge marker in both. Understanding GNR Raman provides a direct bridge to nanotube spectroscopy.
