Crystal structure

Crystal structure — from Kittel Ch. 1

Periodic array of atoms

A crystal is a solid whose atoms are arranged in a pattern that repeats periodically. The entire structure is built by repeating one small unit.

Lattice translation vectors

The periodicity is described by translation vectors a1,a2\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2a1​,a2​. Any translation

T=n1a1+n2a2(n1,n2∈Z)\mathbf{T} = n_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + n_2 \mathbf{a}_2 \quad (n_1, n_2 \in \mathbb{Z})T=n1​a1​+n2​a2​(n1​,n2​∈Z)

maps the crystal onto itself. The set of all such points forms the lattice.

Basis and the crystal structure

Crystal structure=Lattice+Basis\text{Crystal structure} = \text{Lattice} + \text{Basis}Crystal structure=Lattice+Basis

The lattice is an abstract grid of points. The basis is the atom (or group of atoms) placed at each lattice point. Together they make the crystal.

Primitive lattice cell

A primitive cell contains exactly one lattice point and tiles all of space. The Wigner–Seitz cell (right) is the region closer to one point than to any other — it has the full symmetry of the lattice.

2D crystal systems

Lattices are first classified by their point-group symmetry into crystal systems. In 2D there are exactly 4 crystal systems:

Oblique (a1≠a2,  ϕ≠90°a_1 \neq a_2,\; \phi \neq 90°a1​=a2​,ϕ=90°) — Rectangular (a1≠a2,  ϕ=90°a_1 \neq a_2,\; \phi = 90°a1​=a2​,ϕ=90°) — Square (a1=a2,  ϕ=90°a_1 = a_2,\; \phi = 90°a1​=a2​,ϕ=90°) — Hexagonal (a1=a2,  ϕ=120°a_1 = a_2,\; \phi = 120°a1​=a2​,ϕ=120°)

The crystal system determines the symmetry constraints. Within a crystal system, there may be more than one Bravais lattice.

2D Bravais lattices

Within the 4 crystal systems there are exactly 5 Bravais lattice types. The rectangular system admits two: primitive (p) and centered (c).

Every other system has only one Bravais lattice: oblique (p), square (p), hexagonal (p). Graphene belongs to the hexagonal system.

Graphene: honeycomb = hexagonal lattice + 2-atom basis

a1=a(1, 0),a2=a ⁣(12, 32)\mathbf{a}_1 = a(1,\,0), \quad \mathbf{a}_2 = a\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)a1​=a(1,0),a2​=a(21​,23​​) dA=0,dB=a3(0, 1)\mathbf{d}_A = \mathbf{0}, \quad \mathbf{d}_B = \tfrac{a}{\sqrt{3}}(0,\,1)dA​=0,dB​=3​a​(0,1)
The honeycomb is not a Bravais lattice — A and B sites have different neighbor orientations. It is a hexagonal Bravais lattice (∣a1∣=∣a2∣,  ϕ=60°|\mathbf{a}_1| = |\mathbf{a}_2|,\; \phi = 60°∣a1​∣=∣a2​∣,ϕ=60°) with a two-atom basis.

Reciprocal lattice

The reciprocal lattice is the Fourier dual of the real lattice, defined by ai⋅bj=2πδij\mathbf{a}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}_j = 2\pi\delta_{ij}ai​⋅bj​=2πδij​:

b1=2πa ⁣(1, −13),b2=2πa ⁣(0, 23)\mathbf{b}_1 = \tfrac{2\pi}{a}\!\left(1,\,-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right), \quad \mathbf{b}_2 = \tfrac{2\pi}{a}\!\left(0,\,\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right)b1​=a2π​(1,−3​1​),b2​=a2π​(0,3​2​)

Brillouin zone

The first Brillouin zone is the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice. All distinct electron states live inside it.

K=4π3a(1, 0),K′=4π3a(−1, 0)\mathbf{K} = \tfrac{4\pi}{3a}(1,\,0), \quad \mathbf{K}^\prime = \tfrac{4\pi}{3a}(-1,\,0)K=3a4π​(1,0),K′=3a4π​(−1,0)
In graphene, the valence and conduction bands touch at K and K' with zero gap → Dirac cones.
Derivation

Tight-binding band structure of graphene

What is the tight-binding model?

In a crystal, electrons belong to individual atoms but can "hop" to neighboring atoms through the overlap of their atomic orbitals. The tight-binding model is the simplest quantum-mechanical way to describe this: we assume each electron is tightly bound to one atom, and only occasionally hops to the nearest neighbor.

Think of it like a ball on a spring: the electron mostly stays near "its" atom, but quantum mechanics allows it to tunnel to the next atom. How easy this tunneling is determines how the electrons move through the crystal — and thus the material's electrical properties.

The key parameter is the hopping energy ttt: the energy gained when an electron hops from one atom to its neighbor. For graphene's carbon pz_zz​ orbitals, t≈2.7t \approx 2.7t≈2.7 eV. A larger ttt means easier hopping and wider energy bands.

The goal: start from this hopping picture and derive the energy–momentum relation E(k)E(\mathbf{k})E(k) (the "band structure") that tells us which electron energies are allowed at each crystal momentum k\mathbf{k}k.

Step 1 — The honeycomb lattice

Graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a honeycomb pattern. The honeycomb is not a Bravais lattice on its own — it is a hexagonal Bravais lattice with a two-atom basis — sublattices A and B. Each unit cell therefore contains exactly two atoms.

With lattice constant aaa (the distance between equivalent atoms), the primitive lattice vectors are

a1=a ⁣(1, 0),a2=a ⁣(12, 32)\mathbf{a}_1 = a\!\left(1,\,0\right), \qquad \mathbf{a}_2 = a\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)a1​=a(1,0),a2​=a(21​,23​​)

Every A atom has three nearest B neighbors, separated by vectors

δ1=a3 ⁣(0, 1),δ2=a3 ⁣( ⁣−32, −12),δ3=a3 ⁣(32, −12)\boldsymbol{\delta}_1 = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}\!\left(0,\,1\right), \quad \boldsymbol{\delta}_2 = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}\!\left(\!-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2}\right), \quad \boldsymbol{\delta}_3 = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}\!\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)δ1​=3​a​(0,1),δ2​=3​a​(−23​​,−21​),δ3​=3​a​(23​​,−21​)
The carbon–carbon bond length is a/3≈1.42a/\sqrt{3} \approx 1.42a/3​≈1.42 Å. These three vectors δj\boldsymbol{\delta}_jδj​ point from any A atom to its three B neighbors — they are the "hopping paths".

Step 2 — Writing the Hamiltonian

Each carbon atom contributes one pz_zz​ orbital (perpendicular to the graphene sheet). In the tight-binding model, an electron sitting on an A atom can hop to a neighboring B atom with energy ttt, and vice versa. We write this as the Hamiltonian:

H=−t∑⟨i,j⟩(a^i†b^j+b^j†a^i)H = -t \sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} \bigl(\hat{a}_i^\dagger \hat{b}_j + \hat{b}_j^\dagger \hat{a}_i\bigr)H=−t⟨i,j⟩∑​(a^i†​b^j​+b^j†​a^i​)
a^i†\hat{a}_i^\daggera^i†​ creates an electron on A-site iii; b^j\hat{b}_jb^j​ destroys one on B-site jjj. The first term moves an electron from B to A; the second from A to B. The sum ⟨i,j⟩\langle i,j\rangle⟨i,j⟩ runs over all nearest-neighbor pairs. The minus sign is a convention that makes t>0t > 0t>0.

Step 3 — Using Bloch's theorem

Because the lattice repeats periodically, we can label each electron state by a crystal momentum k\mathbf{k}k (a wave vector inside the Brillouin zone). This is Bloch's theorem: the wavefunction picks up a phase eik⋅Re^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}eik⋅R when translated by a lattice vector R\mathbf{R}R.

We write the trial wavefunction as a sum over all unit cells:

∣ψk⟩=cA∑Reik⋅R∣ϕA(R)⟩+cB∑Reik⋅R∣ϕB(R)⟩|\psi_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle = c_A \sum_{\mathbf{R}} e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}|\phi_A(\mathbf{R})\rangle + c_B \sum_{\mathbf{R}} e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}|\phi_B(\mathbf{R})\rangle∣ψk​⟩=cA​R∑​eik⋅R∣ϕA​(R)⟩+cB​R∑​eik⋅R∣ϕB​(R)⟩
Here cAc_AcA​ and cBc_BcB​ are unknown coefficients — how much of the electron's wavefunction lives on sublattice A vs. B. Bloch's theorem reduces the infinite crystal to a single unit cell problem, parametrized by k\mathbf{k}k.

Plugging this into H∣ψ⟩=E∣ψ⟩H|\psi\rangle = E|\psi\rangleH∣ψ⟩=E∣ψ⟩ turns the problem into finding the eigenvalues of a small 2×2 matrix (one row per sublattice).

Step 4 — The 2×2 Bloch Hamiltonian

After projecting onto the two sublattice Bloch sums, the Schrödinger equation becomes

H(k)(cAcB)=E(cAcB)H(\mathbf{k}) \begin{pmatrix} c_A \\ c_B \end{pmatrix} = E \begin{pmatrix} c_A \\ c_B \end{pmatrix}H(k)(cA​cB​​)=E(cA​cB​​)

H(k)=(0−t f(k)−t f∗(k)0)H(\mathbf{k}) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -t\,f(\mathbf{k}) \\[6pt] -t\,f^*(\mathbf{k}) & 0 \end{pmatrix}H(k)=(0−tf∗(k)​−tf(k)0​)

The diagonal entries are zero (A↔A and B↔B hopping is absent in the nearest-neighbor model). All the physics is in the off-diagonal structure factor:

f(k)=∑j=13e ik⋅δjf(\mathbf{k}) = \sum_{j=1}^{3} e^{\,i\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}_j}f(k)=j=1∑3​eik⋅δj​

Step 5 — Evaluating the structure factor

Substituting the three nearest-neighbor vectors δj\boldsymbol{\delta}_jδj​ and writing k=(kx,ky)\mathbf{k} = (k_x, k_y)k=(kx​,ky​):

Step 6 — The energy bands

The eigenvalues of the 2×2 matrix are found from the characteristic equation det⁡(H−E 1)=0\det(H - E\,\mathbb{1}) = 0det(H−E1)=0:

E2−t2 ∣f(k)∣2=0E^2 - t^2\,|f(\mathbf{k})|^2 = 0E2−t2∣f(k)∣2=0

which immediately gives the two energy bands:

  E±(k)=± t ∣f(k)∣  \boxed{\; E_{\pm}(\mathbf{k}) = \pm\, t\,|f(\mathbf{k})| \;}E±​(k)=±t∣f(k)∣​
The +++ branch is the conduction band (π*), the −-− branch is the valence band (π). They are mirror images — this perfect electron–hole symmetry comes from having only A↔B hopping. The bands touch (E=0E = 0E=0) wherever f(k)=0f(\mathbf{k}) = 0f(k)=0.

Step 7 — Where do the bands touch? The Dirac points

Setting f(k)=0f(\mathbf{k}) = 0f(k)=0 and solving, the zeros lie at the corners of the hexagonal Brillouin zone:

K=4π3a(1, 0),K′=4π3a(−1, 0)\mathbf{K} = \frac{4\pi}{3a}(1,\,0), \qquad \mathbf{K}^\prime = \frac{4\pi}{3a}(-1,\,0)K=3a4π​(1,0),K′=3a4π​(−1,0)

There are six corners in total, but they group into just two inequivalent points: K and K′ (the other four are copies related by reciprocal lattice vectors). At these points, E=0E = 0E=0 — the conduction and valence bands touch with zero gap.

This is why graphene is called a zero-gap semiconductor (or semimetal): there is no energy gap between the filled and empty states, but the density of states vanishes at the touching point.

Step 8 — Zoom in: the Dirac equation

To understand the physics near a Dirac point, let k=K+q\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{K} + \mathbf{q}k=K+q where q\mathbf{q}q is small. Taylor-expanding fff to first order in q\mathbf{q}q:

f(K+q)≈3 a2(qx+i qy)+O(q2)f(\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{q}) \approx \frac{\sqrt{3}\,a}{2}(q_x + i\,q_y) + \mathcal{O}(q^2)f(K+q)≈23​a​(qx​+iqy​)+O(q2)

Substituting back, the 2×2 Hamiltonian near K becomes

Heff=ℏvF(0qx−i qyqx+i qy0)=ℏvF (σ⋅q)H_{\text{eff}} = \hbar v_F \begin{pmatrix} 0 & q_x - i\,q_y \\[6pt] q_x + i\,q_y & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \hbar v_F\,(\boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{q})Heff​=ℏvF​(0qx​+iqy​​qx​−iqy​0​)=ℏvF​(σ⋅q)

where σ=(σx,σy)\boldsymbol{\sigma} = (\sigma_x, \sigma_y)σ=(σx​,σy​) are the Pauli matrices, and

vF=3 t a2ℏ≈106  m/sv_F = \frac{\sqrt{3}\,t\,a}{2\hbar} \approx 10^6 \;\text{m/s}vF​=2ℏ3​ta​≈106m/s
This is the 2D massless Dirac equation — the same equation that describes massless relativistic particles, but with the speed of light replaced by vFv_FvF​ (about 300× slower). The Pauli matrices act on the sublattice (A/B) degree of freedom, called pseudospin.

Step 9 — The linear (conical) dispersion

The eigenvalues of HeffH_{\text{eff}}Heff​ give the famous linear dispersion:

  E±(q)=± ℏvF∣q∣  \boxed{\; E_{\pm}(\mathbf{q}) = \pm\,\hbar v_F |\mathbf{q}| \;}E±​(q)=±ℏvF​∣q∣​

Energy is proportional to ∣q∣|\mathbf{q}|∣q∣ — not ∣q∣2|\mathbf{q}|^2∣q∣2 as in ordinary metals. In 2D, this traces out a cone (the Dirac cone you can see in Module 02 above).

Consequences of the linear dispersion:
• Electrons behave as massless Dirac fermions with "speed of light" vFv_FvF​.
• The effective mass is zero → ultra-high mobility.
• The density of states vanishes linearly at E=0E = 0E=0.
• The pseudospin is locked to the momentum direction → Klein tunneling (perfect transmission through barriers).
