How Raman Spectroscopy Works

Raman scattering reveals the vibrational fingerprint of a material — a workhorse technique in 2D materials and condensed matter physics. Explore the physics below through three interactive views: the classical origin of the signal, the quantum energy-level picture, and the molecular vibrations themselves.

01 — Classical picture

Vibration modulates the induced dipole

An incident light wave E(t) drives an oscillating dipole in the molecule, p(t) = α(t)·E(t). Because the molecular vibration periodically changes the polarizability α, the dipole becomes amplitude-modulated. That modulation creates two sidebands around the laser frequency — the Stokes and anti-Stokes Raman lines. Drag the sliders and watch the spectrum respond.

Incident fieldE(t) = E₀ cos(ω₀t)
Polarizabilityα(t) = α₀ + α′·q(t)
Induced dipolep(t) = α(t)·E(t)

02 — Quantum picture

Energy-level (Jablonski) diagram

A photon excites the molecule to a short-lived virtual state, then a photon is scattered as it relaxes. Where it lands sets the scattered energy: same level (Rayleigh), a higher vibrational level (Stokes, red-shifted), or back down from an excited level (anti-Stokes, blue-shifted).

03 — Molecular vibrations

Normal modes of a linear triatomic (e.g. CO₂)

Only vibrations that change the molecule's polarizability are Raman-active. For a centrosymmetric molecule, the symmetric stretch is Raman-active while the asymmetric stretch and bend are IR-active (the mutual exclusion rule). Pick a mode to see it move.
