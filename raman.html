01

How Raman Spectroscopy Works

Interactive guide

Raman scattering probes the vibrational fingerprint of a material: an incident photon is inelastically scattered by a lattice vibration, and the frequency shift $\omega_0\!\pm\!\omega_k$ maps directly onto phonon energies. This page builds the effect from a single starting point — the polarizability $\alpha$ modulated by a normal coordinate $Q_k$ — and follows it through to the angle-resolved selection rules used to fingerprint crystal symmetry (here, the orthorhombic Cmcm rare-earth tritellurides RTe$_3$).

00 — What is polarizability?

Light displaces the electron cloud

An oscillating light field $\mathbf{E}(t)$ displaces the negative electron cloud against the positive nucleus, inducing a dipole moment $\boldsymbol{\mu}=\alpha\,\mathbf{E}$. The proportionality constant — the polarizability $\alpha$ — quantifies how easily the cloud deforms. It is the single quantity from which the entire Raman effect follows.

A static $\alpha$ gives a dipole oscillating at the drive frequency $\omega_0$ — elastic Rayleigh scattering. The Raman effect appears only when $\alpha$ is itself modulated by a lattice vibration.

$\alpha$ is a symmetric second-rank tensor. Each field component induces a dipole in every direction: $$\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{xx}&\alpha_{xy}&\alpha_{xz}\\[2pt]\alpha_{xy}&\alpha_{yy}&\alpha_{yz}\\[2pt]\alpha_{xz}&\alpha_{yz}&\alpha_{zz}\end{pmatrix},\qquad \alpha_{\rho\sigma}=\alpha_{\sigma\rho}.$$ Because $\alpha$ depends on the nuclear positions, expand it to first order in the normal coordinate $Q_k$ (phonon $k$): $$\alpha_{\rho\sigma}(Q_k)=(\alpha_0)_{\rho\sigma}+\sum_k\!\left(\frac{\partial\alpha_{\rho\sigma}}{\partial Q_k}\right)_{\!0}\!Q_k .$$ The derivative tensor $R_k=(\partial\alpha_{\rho\sigma}/\partial Q_k)_0$ is the Raman tensor. Everything below follows from whether it vanishes.
01 — From molecular vibration to the Raman signal

How a vibration modulates the polarizability

Pick a normal mode of CO2. The molecule vibrates (top-left) and the $\alpha(Q)$ curve (top-right) tracks the polarizability along the vibrational coordinate. Only a non-zero slope $\partial\alpha/\partial Q\neq 0$ at equilibrium produces a first-order modulation — and therefore Raman sidebands.

The classical effect in four steps. Drive the molecule with a monochromatic field and let one mode oscillate: $$\mathbf{E}(t)=E_0\,\mathbf{e}_i\cos\omega_0 t,\qquad Q_k(t)=Q_{k0}\cos\omega_k t .$$ Insert the expansion of $\alpha$ into $\boldsymbol\mu=\alpha\mathbf E$ and apply the product-to-sum identity. The induced dipole splits into three frequencies: $$\mu_\rho=\underbrace{(\alpha_0)_{\rho\sigma}E_0 e_{i\sigma}\cos\omega_0 t}_{\text{Rayleigh } \omega_0} +\tfrac12\,(R_k)_{\rho\sigma}E_0 e_{i\sigma}\Big[\underbrace{\cos(\omega_0-\omega_k)t}_{\text{Stokes}}+\underbrace{\cos(\omega_0+\omega_k)t}_{\text{anti-Stokes}}\Big].$$ The Stokes/anti-Stokes sidebands carry the prefactor $R_k$ — they exist iff the Raman tensor is non-zero.

Selection rule. A mode is Raman-active iff at least one Raman-tensor component is non-zero: $$\exists\,(\rho,\sigma):\ \ (R_k)_{\rho\sigma}=\left(\frac{\partial\alpha_{\rho\sigma}}{\partial Q_k}\right)_0\neq 0 .$$ Which components survive is fixed entirely by the point/space-group symmetry. Centrosymmetric systems obey mutual exclusion: the symmetric stretch modulates $\alpha$ (Raman-active, IR-inactive); the asymmetric stretch modulates $\boldsymbol\mu$ (IR-active, Raman-inactive).
Scattering intensity. Radiating the induced dipole (Maxwell, far field) and squaring gives $$I_s \;\propto\; I_i\,\big|\mathbf{e}_s\!\cdot R_k\cdot\mathbf{e}_i\big|^2\,\omega_s^4 .$$ Three factors: incident intensity $I_i$, the projected Raman tensor $|\mathbf e_s\!\cdot R_k\cdot\mathbf e_i|^2$ (sets symmetry & polarization rules), and the $\omega_s^4$ scattered-frequency dependence — why shorter-wavelength lasers scatter more strongly. Per photon, anti-Stokes ($\omega_0+\omega_k$) is favored by $\omega_s^4$, yet Stokes dominates because the $v\!=\!0$ initial state is far more populated (Boltzmann).
02 — Quantum picture

Energy-level (Jablonski) diagram

A photon excites the molecule to a short-lived virtual state, then a photon is scattered as it relaxes. Where it lands sets the scattered energy: same level (Rayleigh), a higher vibrational level (Stokes, red-shifted), or back down from an excited level (anti-Stokes, blue-shifted).

Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio and temperature. At thermal equilibrium, most molecules sit in the ground vibrational state (v = 0). Stokes scattering starts from v = 0 (abundant), while anti-Stokes starts from v = 1 (thermally populated). Their intensity ratio $I_\text{aS}/I_\text{S} \propto \exp(-\hbar\omega_v / k_B T)$ directly encodes the sample temperature — a principle widely used in non-contact thermometry. At low temperatures, anti-Stokes peaks nearly vanish; at high temperatures, they grow toward the Stokes intensity.
03 — From molecules to crystals: angle-resolved Raman

Raman tensors & polarization dependence in RTe$_3$

In a crystal, vibrations are collective phonon modes labelled by the irreducible representations of the space group. Each Raman-active phonon carries a Raman tensor $R$ of symmetry-fixed form, and rotating the light polarization ($\theta$) maps out $|\mathbf e_s\!\cdot R\cdot\mathbf e_i|^2$ — a direct fingerprint of mode symmetry. Below, the worked example is the orthorhombic rare-earth tritelluride family RTe$_3$.

Step 1 — Mode count from symmetry

RTe$_3$ crystallizes in space group Cmcm (No. 63) with every ion on a $c$ Wyckoff site. The factor-group analysis gives the zone-center decomposition

$$\Gamma = 4A_g + 4B_{1g} + 4B_{3g} + B_{1u} + B_{2u} + B_{3u}.$$

The three $g$ (gerade) species — $A_g,\ B_{1g},\ B_{3g}$ — are Raman-active; the $u$ species are IR / acoustic. No $B_{2g}$ modes exist by symmetry.

Step 3 — Geometry of the experiment

RTe$_3$ layers lie in the $ac$-plane, bound by van der Waals gaps along $b$. Backscattering therefore propagates along $b$, and both polarization vectors stay in the $ac$-plane. In the parallel configuration ($\mathbf e_i\!\parallel\!\mathbf e_s$), with $\theta$ the angle from the $a$-axis,

$$\mathbf e_i=\mathbf e_s=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\0\\\sin\theta\end{pmatrix}.$$

Because $\mathbf e$ has no $y$-component, the off-diagonal $B_{1g}$ and $B_{3g}$ tensors give zero — only the $A_g$ mode is observable in this geometry.

Step 4 — Angle dependence of the $A_g$ mode

Evaluating $I_s^{\parallel}\propto|\mathbf e_s\!\cdot R_{A_g}\!\cdot\mathbf e_i|^2$ with the diagonal $A_g$ tensor yields

$$I_s^{\parallel}\propto a^2\cos^4\theta + c^2\sin^4\theta + 2ac\,\cos^2\theta\sin^2\theta\cos\phi,$$

where $\phi$ is the phase difference between the complex Raman-tensor components $a$ and $c$. The polar plot of $I_s^{\parallel}(\theta)$ shows the characteristic two-/four-lobed pattern whose shape fixes the ratio $c/a$ and the phase $\phi$ — the fitting handle used to assign modes and to track the four-fold CDW distortion in RTe$_3$.

Two angle-resolved setups

A half-wave plate rotates the polarization. Type I sweeps the laser polarization while the analyzer angle is fixed; Type II rotates laser and signal together at a fixed parallel (or perpendicular) offset. Type II is the parallel-polarization configuration used for the $A_g$ formula above.

Finding Raman tensors for your crystal

For any crystal structure, the Raman tensor forms are fully determined by the space group symmetry. The Bilbao Crystallographic Server (cryst.ehu.es) provides free, authoritative tools for this:

  • SAM (Site-symmetry Approach to Modes) — decomposes phonon modes into irreducible representations and labels each as Raman-active, IR-active, or silent
  • MODESINF — gives the explicit Raman tensor form for each active mode in a chosen space group
  • POINT — character tables and irreducible representations for any point group

Enter your space group number (e.g. 63 for Cmcm), and the server returns all Raman-active mode symmetries and tensor matrices directly — no manual group theory needed.

What Raman spectra tell you

Beyond identifying materials, Raman spectra encode a wealth of structural and physical information:

  • Peak position — phonon frequency, sensitive to composition and bond strength
  • Peak width — crystallinity (broader = more disorder or smaller grains)
  • Peak shift — strain, temperature, or doping changes
  • Peak intensity — thickness (in thin films/2D materials)
  • Polarization dependence — crystal orientation and mode symmetry
