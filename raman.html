How Raman Spectroscopy Works

Raman scattering reveals the vibrational fingerprint of a material by detecting inelastic scattering — photons that exchange energy with lattice vibrations (phonons). The resulting Stokes shift directly corresponds to the phonon energy, giving access to crystallinity, strain, temperature, thickness, and crystal orientation from a single, non-invasive optical measurement. The key rule: a vibration is Raman-active only if it changes the polarizability. Explore the physics step by step below.

00 — What is polarizability?

Light displaces the electron cloud

An atom has a positive nucleus surrounded by a negative electron cloud. When an oscillating light field E(t) arrives, it pushes the electron cloud away from the nucleus — creating an induced dipole moment μ. The ease of this displacement is the polarizability α. Watch the cloud shift back and forth with the field.

The electron cloud (large, light) moves much more than the heavy nucleus. The induced dipole μ = α · E oscillates at the same frequency as the light — this is Rayleigh scattering. If α itself oscillates (because the molecule vibrates), sidebands appear — that's Raman scattering.

Going deeper — α is a tensor. Polarizability α is a symmetric second-rank tensor (a 3×3 matrix), capturing how each component of the electric field induces a dipole in every direction. When the molecule vibrates along a generalized coordinate Q, the first-order Taylor expansion gives α(Q) ≈ α₀ + (∂α/∂Q)₀ · Q. The derivative (∂α/∂Q)₀ — a component of the Raman tensor — is the heart of the Raman effect: if it is non-zero, the vibration modulates α and produces Raman scattering.

How a vibration modulates the polarizability

Selection rule. A vibration along generalized coordinate Qk is Raman-active when at least one component of its Raman tensor Rk = (∂αij/∂Qk)₀ is non-zero. Which components survive is dictated by the symmetry of the crystal structure (point or space group). For centrosymmetric molecules like CO2, the mutual exclusion rule applies: a mode is either Raman-active or IR-active, never both. The symmetric stretch changes α (Raman-active, IR-inactive), while the asymmetric stretch changes the dipole moment (IR-active, Raman-inactive).
Scattering intensity. The scattered intensity is proportional to I ∝ I₀ · |R|² · ωs⁴ — the incident laser intensity, the square of the Raman tensor element, and the fourth power of the scattered frequency. The ω⁴ dependence explains why shorter-wavelength lasers produce stronger Raman signals. It also means anti-Stokes photons (higher ωs) are intrinsically stronger per photon than Stokes — but since fewer molecules occupy excited vibrational states (Boltzmann statistics), Stokes peaks dominate in practice.
02 — Quantum picture

Energy-level (Jablonski) diagram

A photon excites the molecule to a short-lived virtual state, then a photon is scattered as it relaxes. Where it lands sets the scattered energy: same level (Rayleigh), a higher vibrational level (Stokes, red-shifted), or back down from an excited level (anti-Stokes, blue-shifted).

Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio and temperature. At thermal equilibrium, most molecules sit in the ground vibrational state (v = 0). Stokes scattering starts from v = 0 (abundant), while anti-Stokes starts from v = 1 (thermally populated). Their intensity ratio IaS/IS ∝ exp(−ℏωv/kBT) directly encodes the sample temperature — a principle widely used in non-contact thermometry. At low temperatures, anti-Stokes peaks nearly vanish; at high temperatures, they grow toward the Stokes intensity.
03 — From molecules to crystals

Raman tensors & polarization dependence

In a crystal, vibrations are collective phonon modes classified by the crystal's space group symmetry. Each Raman-active phonon has a specific Raman tensor whose form is fixed by symmetry — and the scattered intensity depends on polarization directions.

Polarization-dependent intensity

The Raman intensity for a given mode is:

I ∝ |ei · R · es|²

where ei and es are the polarization vectors of incident and scattered light, and R is the Raman tensor of the phonon mode. By rotating the polarization angle (angle-resolved Raman spectroscopy), one can determine the symmetry of each mode — distinguishing Ag, B1g, B2g modes, etc.

What Raman spectra tell you

Beyond identifying materials, Raman spectra encode a wealth of structural and physical information:

  • Peak position — phonon frequency, sensitive to composition and bond strength
  • Peak width — crystallinity (broader = more disorder or smaller grains)
  • Peak shift — strain, temperature, or doping changes
  • Peak intensity — thickness (in thin films/2D materials)
  • Polarization dependence — crystal orientation and mode symmetry
