Graphene Electronic Structureグラフェン電子構造

Peel a single atomic layer off a pencil lead, and you have graphene — a sheet of carbon one atom thick. Inside it, electrons do something extraordinary: they move as if they had no mass at all. Why? The answer is hidden in the geometry of the honeycomb. This page follows the trail — lattice → Brillouin zone → band structure — until the Dirac cones appear.鉛筆の芯から、炭素の層をたった一枚だけ剥がしたもの — それがグラフェンです。厚さ原子1個分のこのシートの中で、電子は驚くべき振る舞いをします。まるで質量を持たないかのように飛び回るのです。なぜそんなことが起こるのでしょうか？ 答えはハニカム（蜂の巣）模様の幾何学に隠れています。格子 → ブリルアンゾーン → バンド構造と手がかりをたどっていくと、その先にディラック錐が姿を現します。

Honeycomb lattice & Brillouin zoneハニカム格子とブリルアンゾーン

At first glance, the honeycomb looks like the simplest pattern in the world. But try sliding the lattice onto itself, and you notice something odd: not every atom sees the same view. Half the atoms (A) have their three neighbors pointing one way; the other half (B), the opposite way. So the honeycomb is not a Bravais lattice by itself — it is a hexagonal lattice with a two-atom basis, and every unit cell holds exactly one A and one B. Remember this “two-ness”: it is the key to everything that follows. Now switch to reciprocal space. The first Brillouin zone is a hexagon, and its corners — the K and K′ points — look unremarkable for now. But that is exactly where graphene's strangest physics is waiting. Dirac cones and the high-symmetry pathディラック錐と高対称経路

Here comes the payoff. In the nearest-neighbour tight-binding model, graphene's entire band structure collapses into one formula: E(k) = ±t |f(k)|, where t ≈ 2.7 eV is the energy for an electron to hop between adjacent pz orbitals, and f(k) measures how the three hopping paths interfere. Wherever the three paths cancel perfectly, f(k) = 0 — and the two bands touch. That happens precisely at the K/K′ corners of the Brillouin zone, producing the six Dirac cones on the full E(kx, ky) surface (right): the conduction (π*) and valence (π) bands meet with zero gap, and near each tip the dispersion is a straight line. A linear E–k relation is what a massless particle obeys — this is why graphene's electrons move as if they weigh nothing. The line plot (left) is the same surface cut along the high-symmetry path Γ→M→K→Γ, highlighted in blue on the surface. Crystal structure — a crash course (after Kittel, Ch. 1)

Periodic array of atoms

Before we can say anything about electrons in graphene, we need a language for describing crystals. The starting point could not be simpler: a crystal is a solid whose atoms are arranged in a pattern that repeats periodically. Describe one small repeating unit, and you have described the entire — possibly infinite — solid. That economy is the whole point of crystallography.

Lattice translation vectors

How do we say “it repeats” in mathematics? With translation vectors a1,a2\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2a1​,a2​. Any translation

T=n1a1+n2a2(n1,n2∈Z)\mathbf{T} = n_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + n_2 \mathbf{a}_2 \quad (n_1, n_2 \in \mathbb{Z})T=n1​a1​+n2​a2​(n1​,n2​∈Z)

maps the crystal onto itself. The set of all such points forms the lattice.

Basis and the crystal structure

Crystal structure=Lattice+Basis\text{Crystal structure} = \text{Lattice} + \text{Basis}Crystal structure=Lattice+Basis

Notice the division of labor here. The lattice is an abstract grid of points — it says where things go. The basis is the atom (or group of atoms) placed at each lattice point — it says what goes there. Keep this distinction in mind: graphene is about to give the basis a starring role.

Primitive lattice cell

A primitive cell is the smallest tile: it contains exactly one lattice point and fills all of space with no gaps. There are many ways to choose it, but one choice is special — the Wigner–Seitz cell (right), the region closer to one lattice point than to any other. It has the full symmetry of the lattice, and it will make a second appearance soon, in reciprocal space.

2D crystal systems

How many genuinely different lattices can exist in two dimensions? Start by classifying them by their point-group symmetry into crystal systems. In 2D there are exactly 4:

Oblique (a1≠a2,  ϕ≠90°a_1 \neq a_2,\; \phi \neq 90°a1​=a2​,ϕ=90°) — Rectangular (a1≠a2,  ϕ=90°a_1 \neq a_2,\; \phi = 90°a1​=a2​,ϕ=90°) — Square (a1=a2,  ϕ=90°a_1 = a_2,\; \phi = 90°a1​=a2​,ϕ=90°) — Hexagonal (a1=a2,  ϕ=120°a_1 = a_2,\; \phi = 120°a1​=a2​,ϕ=120°)

The crystal system determines the symmetry constraints. Within a crystal system, there may be more than one Bravais lattice.

2D Bravais lattices

Refine the classification by how lattice points can be arranged, and the 4 systems yield exactly 5 Bravais lattice types — that is the complete list; there is no sixth. Only the rectangular system admits two: primitive (p) and centered (c).

Every other system has only one Bravais lattice: oblique (p), square (p), hexagonal (p). Graphene belongs to the hexagonal system.

Graphene: honeycomb = hexagonal lattice + 2-atom basis

a1=a(1, 0),a2=a ⁣(12, 32)\mathbf{a}_1 = a(1,\,0), \quad \mathbf{a}_2 = a\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)a1​=a(1,0),a2​=a(21​,23​​) dA=0,dB=a3(0, 1)\mathbf{d}_A = \mathbf{0}, \quad \mathbf{d}_B = \tfrac{a}{\sqrt{3}}(0,\,1)dA​=0,dB​=3​a​(0,1)
Here is the twist: the honeycomb is not on the list of five! It cannot be a Bravais lattice — A and B sites see their neighbors in opposite orientations, so no single grid of equivalent points captures every atom. The resolution: a hexagonal Bravais lattice (∣a1∣=∣a2∣,  ϕ=60°|\mathbf{a}_1| = |\mathbf{a}_2|,\; \phi = 60°∣a1​∣=∣a2​∣,ϕ=60°) carrying a two-atom basis. Lattice + basis, exactly as advertised.

Reciprocal lattice

Electrons in a crystal are waves, and waves are best labeled by wave vectors. The natural home for those wave vectors is the reciprocal lattice — the Fourier dual of the real lattice, defined by ai⋅bj=2πδij\mathbf{a}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}_j = 2\pi\delta_{ij}ai​⋅bj​=2πδij​:

b1=2πa ⁣(1, −13),b2=2πa ⁣(0, 23)\mathbf{b}_1 = \tfrac{2\pi}{a}\!\left(1,\,-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right), \quad \mathbf{b}_2 = \tfrac{2\pi}{a}\!\left(0,\,\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right)b1​=a2π​(1,−3​1​),b2​=a2π​(0,3​2​)

Brillouin zone

Remember the Wigner–Seitz cell? Construct it in the reciprocal lattice, and you get the first Brillouin zone — the patch of k-space that contains every physically distinct electron state. For graphene's hexagonal lattice it is a hexagon, and its corners are about to become famous.

K=4π3a(1, 0),K′=4π3a(−1, 0)\mathbf{K} = \tfrac{4\pi}{3a}(1,\,0), \quad \mathbf{K}^\prime = \tfrac{4\pi}{3a}(-1,\,0)K=3a4π​(1,0),K′=3a4π​(−1,0)
In graphene, the valence and conduction bands touch at K and K' with zero gap → Dirac cones.

Tight-binding band structure of graphene

What is the tight-binding model?

An electron in a crystal faces a dilemma: it belongs to its own atom, yet the neighboring atoms sit temptingly close, and their orbitals overlap. Quantum mechanics resolves the dilemma in its usual style — the electron stays mostly at home, but every so often it “hops” to a neighbor. The tight-binding model takes this picture literally: each electron is tightly bound to one atom and hops only to its nearest neighbors. Remarkably, this one assumption is enough to produce graphene's entire band structure.

Think of it like a ball on a spring: the electron mostly stays near "its" atom, but quantum mechanics allows it to tunnel to the next atom. How easy this tunneling is determines how the electrons move through the crystal — and thus the material's electrical properties.

The key parameter is the hopping energy ttt: the energy gained when an electron hops from one atom to its neighbor. For graphene's carbon pz_zz​ orbitals, t≈2.7t \approx 2.7t≈2.7 eV. A larger ttt means easier hopping and wider energy bands.

The plan for the nine steps ahead: start from this hopping picture and derive the energy–momentum relation E(k)E(\mathbf{k})E(k) (the "band structure") that tells us which electron energies are allowed at each crystal momentum k\mathbf{k}k.

Step 1 — The honeycomb lattice

First, set the stage. Graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a honeycomb pattern. As we saw in the crystal-structure notes, the honeycomb is not a Bravais lattice on its own — it is a hexagonal Bravais lattice with a two-atom basis, sublattices A and B. Each unit cell therefore contains exactly two atoms. File that “two” away: in Step 4 it will become the size of our matrix.

With lattice constant aaa (the distance between equivalent atoms), the primitive lattice vectors are

a1=a ⁣(1, 0),a2=a ⁣(12, 32)\mathbf{a}_1 = a\!\left(1,\,0\right), \qquad \mathbf{a}_2 = a\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)a1​=a(1,0),a2​=a(21​,23​​)

Every A atom talks to exactly three nearest B neighbors, reached by the vectors

δ1=a3 ⁣(0, 1),δ2=a3 ⁣( ⁣−32, −12),δ3=a3 ⁣(32, −12)\boldsymbol{\delta}_1 = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}\!\left(0,\,1\right), \quad \boldsymbol{\delta}_2 = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}\!\left(\!-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2}\right), \quad \boldsymbol{\delta}_3 = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}\!\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)δ1​=3​a​(0,1),δ2​=3​a​(−23​​,−21​),δ3​=3​a​(23​​,−21​)
The carbon–carbon bond length is a/3≈1.42a/\sqrt{3} \approx 1.42a/3​≈1.42 Å. These three vectors δj\boldsymbol{\delta}_jδj​ point from any A atom to its three B neighbors — they are the "hopping paths".

Step 2 — Writing the Hamiltonian

Now translate the picture into an equation. Each carbon atom contributes one pz_zz​ orbital, sticking out perpendicular to the sheet. An electron sitting on an A atom can hop to a neighboring B atom with energy ttt, and vice versa — and that is all that happens in this model. The Hamiltonian simply writes this down:

H=−t∑⟨i,j⟩(a^i†b^j+b^j†a^i)H = -t \sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} \bigl(\hat{a}_i^\dagger \hat{b}_j + \hat{b}_j^\dagger \hat{a}_i\bigr)H=−t⟨i,j⟩∑​(a^i†​b^j​+b^j†​a^i​)
a^i†\hat{a}_i^\daggera^i†​ creates an electron on A-site iii; b^j\hat{b}_jb^j​ destroys one on B-site jjj. The first term moves an electron from B to A; the second from A to B. The sum ⟨i,j⟩\langle i,j\rangle⟨i,j⟩ runs over all nearest-neighbor pairs. The minus sign is a convention that makes t>0t > 0t>0.

Step 3 — Using Bloch's theorem

Writing down the Hamiltonian was easy; diagonalizing it over infinitely many atoms sounds hopeless. Here the periodicity comes to the rescue. Because the lattice repeats, we can label each electron state by a crystal momentum k\mathbf{k}k (a wave vector inside the Brillouin zone). This is Bloch's theorem: the wavefunction picks up a phase eik⋅Re^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}eik⋅R when translated by a lattice vector R\mathbf{R}R.

We write the trial wavefunction as a sum over all unit cells:

∣ψk⟩=cA∑Reik⋅R∣ϕA(R)⟩+cB∑Reik⋅R∣ϕB(R)⟩|\psi_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle = c_A \sum_{\mathbf{R}} e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}|\phi_A(\mathbf{R})\rangle + c_B \sum_{\mathbf{R}} e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}|\phi_B(\mathbf{R})\rangle∣ψk​⟩=cA​R∑​eik⋅R∣ϕA​(R)⟩+cB​R∑​eik⋅R∣ϕB​(R)⟩
Here cAc_AcA​ and cBc_BcB​ are unknown coefficients — how much of the electron's wavefunction lives on sublattice A vs. B. Bloch's theorem reduces the infinite crystal to a single unit cell problem, parametrized by k\mathbf{k}k.

Plugging this into H∣ψ⟩=E∣ψ⟩H|\psi\rangle = E|\psi\rangleH∣ψ⟩=E∣ψ⟩ collapses the infinite crystal into a small 2×2 matrix problem — one row per sublattice. There is that “two” again.

After projecting onto the two sublattice Bloch sums, the Schrödinger equation becomes

Time to compute. Substituting the three nearest-neighbor vectors δj\boldsymbol{\delta}_jδj​ and writing k=(kx,ky)\mathbf{k} = (k_x, k_y)k=(kx​,ky​):

Step 6 — The energy bands

A 2×2 matrix keeps its promises: the eigenvalues follow in one line from the characteristic equation det⁡(H−E 1)=0\det(H - E\,\mathbb{1}) = 0det(H−E1)=0:

The +++ branch is the conduction band (π*), the −-− branch is the valence band (π). They are mirror images — this perfect electron–hole symmetry comes from having only A↔B hopping. The bands touch (E=0E = 0E=0) wherever f(k)=0f(\mathbf{k}) = 0f(k)=0.

Step 7 — Where do the bands touch? The Dirac points

So where do the bands touch? Setting f(k)=0f(\mathbf{k}) = 0f(k)=0 and solving, we find that the zeros land exactly on the corners of the hexagonal Brillouin zone:

K=4π3a(1, 0),K′=4π3a(−1, 0)\mathbf{K} = \frac{4\pi}{3a}(1,\,0), \qquad \mathbf{K}^\prime = \frac{4\pi}{3a}(-1,\,0)K=3a4π​(1,0),K′=3a4π​(−1,0)

There are six corners in total, but they group into just two inequivalent points: K and K′ (the other four are copies related by reciprocal lattice vectors). At these points, E=0E = 0E=0 — the conduction and valence bands touch with zero gap.

This is why graphene is called a zero-gap semiconductor (or semimetal): there is no energy gap between the filled and empty states, but the density of states vanishes at the touching point.

Step 8 — Zoom in: the Dirac equation

The bands touch at K — but how do they touch? To find out, zoom in. Let k=K+q\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{K} + \mathbf{q}k=K+q where q\mathbf{q}q is small. Taylor-expanding fff to first order in q\mathbf{q}q:

f(K+q)≈3 a2(qx+i qy)+O(q2)f(\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{q}) \approx \frac{\sqrt{3}\,a}{2}(q_x + i\,q_y) + \mathcal{O}(q^2)f(K+q)≈23​a​(qx​+iqy​)+O(q2)

This is the 2D massless Dirac equation — the same equation that describes massless relativistic particles, but with the speed of light replaced by vFv_FvF​ (about 300× slower). The Pauli matrices act on the sublattice (A/B) degree of freedom, called pseudospin.

Step 9 — The linear (conical) dispersion

The eigenvalues of this Hamiltonian HeffH_{\text{eff}}Heff​ deliver the punchline — the famous linear dispersion:

  E±(q)=± ℏvF∣q∣  \boxed{\; E_{\pm}(\mathbf{q}) = \pm\,\hbar v_F |\mathbf{q}| \;}E±​(q)=±ℏvF​∣q∣​

Energy is proportional to ∣q∣|\mathbf{q}|∣q∣ — not ∣q∣2|\mathbf{q}|^2∣q∣2 as for ordinary electrons. A particle whose energy grows linearly with momentum is, by definition, massless. In 2D the relation traces out a cone — the very Dirac cone you can rotate in Module 01 above.

Consequences of the linear dispersion:
• Electrons behave as massless Dirac fermions with "speed of light" vFv_FvF​.
• The effective mass is zero → ultra-high mobility.
• The density of states vanishes linearly at E=0E = 0E=0.
• The pseudospin is locked to the momentum direction → Klein tunneling (perfect transmission through barriers).
